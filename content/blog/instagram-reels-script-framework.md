**The short answer:** a good Reels script has four parts. A hook in the first second, a setup that keeps the promise, a payoff that delivers it, and one next step. Everything you cut makes it better.

I write Reels scripts and plan content calendars as part of my work at Tribe Fortis, and for clients through [Gari Tech](/gari-tech). This is the framework I keep coming back to.

## The four parts

### 1. The hook (first second)

You get about a second before someone scrolls. The hook can be a line, a visual or both. It works when it makes a specific promise or raises a question the viewer wants answered.

- Weak: "Here are some tips about training."
- Stronger: "You do not need a mirror to get fit. Watch this."

Put the hook on the screen as text as well, because many people watch with the sound off.

### 2. The setup (two to five seconds)

One or two lines that say why this matters to the viewer, not to you. Cut anything that sounds like an introduction.

### 3. The payoff (the middle)

Deliver what the hook promised, quickly. If you said three things, show three things. Keep each beat short and change what is on screen often so the eye has something new to follow.

### 4. The next step (the end)

One instruction only: follow, save, comment a word, or visit a link. Two instructions is zero.

## A worked example

This is an illustrative script I made up for a game based fitness class, not a script from a real client.

- **Hook (on screen and spoken):** "No mirrors. No machines. Guess how this class works."
- **Setup:** "It is a sport based workout, so you forget you are exercising."
- **Payoff:** Three quick clips of the group playing, with a caption for each game.
- **Next step:** "Comment PLAY and we will send you the trial timings."

Notice there is nothing in there about the brand being passionate or innovative. Show the thing.

## Editing rules that matter

- Cut every pause that is not doing work.
- Add captions, always.
- Keep the first frame interesting. Do not open on a logo.
- Shoot the payoff first, then write the hook that fits it.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Starting with your name or your brand.
- Making the viewer wait for the point.
- Using a trending sound that fights with your voice.
- Judging a script after one post. Try the same idea three ways before deciding it does not work.

## A bank of hook shapes

Adapt these to your subject. A hook is a shape, not a sentence to copy.

- **The myth:** "You do not need X to get Y."
- **The question:** "Guess how this works."
- **The mistake:** "Stop doing this if you want Z."
- **The result first:** show the finished thing, then explain how.
- **The number:** "Three things I wish I knew before starting."
- **The challenge:** "Can you do this in ten seconds?"
- **The secret:** "The part nobody shows you."

Test two or three shapes on the same idea before deciding what works for your audience.

## Script templates by goal

**To teach:** hook with a promise, three quick steps, one summary line, one next step.

**To build trust:** hook with a common worry, show what really happens, say one honest limit, invite a question.

**To sell:** hook with the problem, show the product solving it in action, one clear offer, one way to act.

**To show culture:** hook with a surprising moment, quick cuts of people, one caption that says why it matters.

## Shot list basics

Before you shoot, write down each shot in a line. A short Reel might have five to eight. Vary distance: a wide shot to set the scene, a close shot for the detail, a movement shot to add life. Shoot a little more than you need. It is easier to cut than to reshoot.

## Captions, text and hashtags

- Add burned in captions so it works with no sound.
- Keep on screen text short and high contrast.
- Write a first line in the caption that adds to the video, not repeats it.
- Use a small number of relevant hashtags rather than a wall of them.
- Say the next step once, clearly.

## How often to post Reels

Once or twice a week is a good start for most small brands. A steady rhythm gives you enough data to see what works, and enough repetition to build a habit with the audience.

## Reading your numbers

Instagram shows you how long people watched and where they dropped off. That drop off point tells you more than the view count.

- If people leave in the first second, your hook is weak.
- If they leave in the middle, the payoff is too slow or too long.
- If they watch to the end but do not act, your next step is unclear.

Change one thing at a time so you know what made the difference.

## Repurposing a Reel

A good Reel can become a carousel, a LinkedIn post, a story and an article section. Write the idea once, and shape it for each place. That is one of the cheapest ways to grow a small brand, and I return to it in the [content strategy plan](/blog/content-marketing-strategy-for-startups).

## Equipment that is enough

A recent phone, natural light near a window, a cheap tripod, and a clip-on microphone if you speak on camera. Good audio matters more than a fancy camera. Editing apps on your phone are enough to start.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I write a Reels script?

Use four parts: a hook in the first second, a short setup, a payoff that delivers the promise, and one next step. Then cut everything that does not help.

### How long should an Instagram Reel be?

As long as it needs to be and no longer. Many good Reels for small brands run from about fifteen to forty five seconds, but a tight short one beats a long loose one.

### What makes a good Reels hook?

A specific promise or a question the viewer wants answered, shown on screen as text as well as spoken.

### How often should I post Reels?

One or two a week is a realistic start. Keep the rhythm before increasing.
