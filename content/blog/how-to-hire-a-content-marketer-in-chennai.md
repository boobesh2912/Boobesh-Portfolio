**The short answer:** to hire a content marketer in Chennai, look at their real work and real reviews first, agree the scope, price and number of changes in writing before they start, begin with a small trial project, and decide on day one which two or three numbers you will track. Where you find them matters less than whether they can show proof.

I should tell you my bias up front. I am Boobesh, aka Boo, a content marketer in Chennai and the founder of [Gari Tech](/gari-tech). So I am one of the people you might hire. I will say where I fit and where I do not, and the rest of this works whoever you hire.

## Freelancer, agency or in-house

There is no best option, only a fit.

- **A freelancer or solo marketer:** you work directly with the person who does the work. It is usually the simplest to start, and the risk is that everything depends on one person.
- **A small agency:** a few people with different skills, such as writing, design and editing. You get more range and some backup, and you pay for the structure.
- **An in-house marketer:** someone who lives inside your business and knows it deeply. It makes sense when you have enough steady work to keep one person busy.

If you are a startup or a small business with one or two channels to build, a freelancer or a small agency is usually the natural starting point. If you have a lot of ongoing work across many channels, in-house starts to make sense. ## What a content marketer should actually do

Before you hire, know what you are buying. A content marketer decides what a brand should say, to whom and in what order, then makes sure it gets made and published. That includes a strategy, a calendar, Reels scripts, LinkedIn posts, newsletters and repurposing. If someone only offers to "post for you", that is social media management, which is different. I explained the difference in [content marketing vs social media marketing](/blog/content-marketing-vs-social-media-marketing).

## What to check before you say yes

1. **Real work you can open.** Live posts, live websites, real case studies. A portfolio of mock-ups says little.
2. **Reviews from people you can find.** Google reviews, LinkedIn recommendations, or a client you can message.
3. **A person, not just a brand.** Can you see who will actually do your work?
4. **How they handle things going wrong.** Ask for one project that did not go well and what they learned. If the answer is "none", be careful.
5. **How they measure.** Ask what they would track for you.

On the last point, I speak from experience. At Tribe Fortis I led content for four months and could not prove what it did, because no one had set up numbers to track. I wrote the whole thing up in the [Tribe Fortis case study](/blog/tribe-fortis-marketing-case-study). Do not repeat that mistake with your own marketer.

## Put four things in writing before work starts

I learned this from the other side, as a freelancer. These four lines prevent most arguments:

- **Scope:** exactly what is included. For example, how many posts, scripts or pages.
- **Price:** and what happens if the work grows.
- **Number of changes:** how many rounds of edits are included.
- **Timeline:** when each piece is due, and when you need to reply.

In my own early work, a project agreed at four pages slowly became nearly nine, and the constant minute-by-minute changes made it stressful for me. Clear scope protects you as well as them.

## Start with a small trial

Before a long contract, ask for a small paid trial, such as a month of posts or one newsletter issue. It shows you how they work, how they communicate and whether their taste matches yours. A good marketer will welcome it. Most of my own clients came through referrals and trials, and it works well for both sides.

## Red flags

- Guaranteed followers, viral reach or rankings. Nobody honest can promise those.
- No live work, no reviews and no names.
- Pressure to sign quickly, or a price that changes on the call.
- Vague deliverables like "we will make your brand grow".
- A marketer who does not ask you any questions about your customers.

## How to brief them

Give them four things: who your customer is, what you sell, what you want people to do after seeing your content, and examples of content you like and dislike. The more specific the brief, the better and cheaper the work.

## Questions people ask

### How much does a content marketer in Chennai cost?

It varies a lot with experience, scope and how many channels you want covered, so I will not quote a number that could mislead you. Ask for a clear scope first, then a price for that scope. Compare proposals on what is included, not only the total.

### Should I hire a freelancer or an agency?

For one or two channels, a freelancer or small agency is often the simpler start. For lots of ongoing work across many channels, you may need a bigger team or an in-house hire.

### How long before content marketing works?

It usually takes months, not days, and anyone who promises faster is selling you something. Decide on a few numbers to track from the first week.

### What should I ask in the first call?

Ask to see live work, ask for one project that went wrong, ask what they would track for you, and ask exactly what is and is not included.

### Is content marketing the same as digital marketing?

No. Digital marketing covers all online channels, including paid ads and SEO. Content marketing is the part that creates useful content to attract and keep an audience.
