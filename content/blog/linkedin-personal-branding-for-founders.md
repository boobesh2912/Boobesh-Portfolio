**The short answer:** personal branding on LinkedIn is a consistency game. Pick one lane, publish on a rhythm you can keep, and be specific about what you know. Cleverness helps, but showing up matters more.

## Why I am writing this

I found LinkedIn after watching a lot of videos. At first I barely used it. Then I slowly started posting, and it became the one platform I stayed consistent on. I still do not fully know why, but it means I know first hand what plain persistence does, and what it does not.

## Step 1: pick a lane

"I post about business" is not a lane. "I explain marketing to college students" is. A lane lets someone decide in two seconds whether to follow you. Choose the one you can talk about for a year without getting bored.

## Step 2: fix your profile before you post

People will visit your profile after a good post, so it is your landing page.

- A photo where your face is clear.
- A headline that says what you do and for whom, not just a job title.
- An About section that answers "why should I listen to you" in plain sentences.
- A featured link to something you actually want people to open.

## Step 3: post on a rhythm

Choose a number you can keep, such as two or three posts a week, and protect it. A steady small rhythm builds more trust than bursts of daily posts followed by silence.

## Step 4: use a few repeatable formats

You do not need a new idea every time. Rotate between a few shapes.

- **A lesson from something you did this week.** Specific, small and true.
- **A mistake and what it taught you.** These get replies, because people relate.
- **A how-to in five short lines.**
- **An opinion, with a reason.** Not a hot take for its own sake.

## Step 5: spend as long commenting as posting

Reply to every comment on your posts, and leave real comments on other people's. This is where most of the relationships come from, and it is the part people skip.

## Step 6: turn it into work

A personal brand only matters if it leads somewhere. Put a clear next step in your profile, and say plainly what you offer.

## What to avoid

- Copying the tone of famous creators.
- Posting inspirational lines with no experience behind them.
- Comparing your week two with someone else's year three.

## A profile checklist, in more detail

Treat your profile like a landing page, and check each part.

- **Headline.** Say who you help and how, in plain words. "Founder of X" tells nobody anything. "I help college students choose careers" does.
- **Photo.** Clear face, good light, friendly. It is the first thing people see next to your comments.
- **Banner.** Use it to say something, such as what you do or where to find you.
- **About section.** Write in the first person, short paragraphs, and end with how to contact you.
- **Featured.** Pin the two or three pages you most want people to open.
- **Experience.** Describe what you did in outcomes, not job titles.
- **Custom URL.** Shorten it to your name so it is easy to share. Mine is linkedin.com/in/boobesh2912.

## Post structures that work

You do not need to invent a format every time. These four cover most weeks.

1. **The lesson:** "This week I learned X. Here is what happened. Here is what I would do next time."
2. **The mistake:** what went wrong, why, and what you changed. People relate to honesty more than polish.
3. **The how-to:** a five step or five line guide to one small task.
4. **The opinion:** "Most people say X. I think Y, because Z." Give a reason, not just a take.

Write the first line last. It decides whether anyone clicks "see more".

## A bank of opening lines

Use these as shapes, not as scripts.

- "I used to think X. I was wrong."
- "The most useful thing I did this month was small."
- "Nobody warns you about this part of starting up."
- "Here is a mistake I keep seeing."
- "I have done this for [a year, a project, a class]. Here is what changed."

## How often to post

Two to three times a week is enough for most people. Choose days and stick to them. Consistency tells the algorithm and your audience what to expect.

## A twenty minute engagement routine

Spend twenty minutes a day on this, and it will matter as much as posting.

1. Reply to every comment on your recent posts.
2. Leave five thoughtful comments on posts from people in your field.
3. Send one useful message to someone you have been commenting with, with no pitch attached.

Comments that add something, an example, a question or a different view, are worth more than a "great post".

## Personal profile or company page?

For a founder or a small business, the personal profile usually gets far more reach than a company page, because people follow people. Use the company page as a place to send people for facts, and keep the personal profile as your voice.

## Common mistakes

- Copying the tone of famous creators.
- Posting inspirational lines with no experience behind them.
- Vague headlines nobody can quote.
- Deleting a post because it got few likes on the first day.
- Selling in every post. Aim for the usual rule: help several times for each time you ask.

## LinkedIn and search

LinkedIn profiles and posts can appear in search results, so use the words people would type. A clear headline, a plain About section and posts about your actual subject help both LinkedIn and Google connect your name with your work. It is one of the reasons I keep my LinkedIn link on every page of this site.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I build a personal brand on LinkedIn?

Pick one lane, fix your profile, post two or three times a week using a few repeatable formats, and spend as much time commenting as posting.

### How often should a founder post on LinkedIn?

Two to three times a week is a sustainable rhythm for most people. Consistency matters more than volume.

### What should I write about on LinkedIn as a founder?

Lessons from your week, mistakes and what you changed, small how-tos, and honest opinions with reasons.

### How long does LinkedIn personal branding take?

Plan for at least three months of steady posting before judging. Trust builds slowly.
