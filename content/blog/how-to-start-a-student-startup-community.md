**The short answer:** to start a student startup community, start tiny with a few founding members, make one clear promise about who it is for, build one repeatable ritual (a weekly or monthly session), and plan from the beginning for who takes over when you get busy. I started one in 2025 and it stopped after four webinars, so this is partly a guide and partly an honest post-mortem.

I am Boobesh, aka Boo, a student and entrepreneur from Chennai. I started **Start The Up**, a student startup community, in 2025. The idea was simple: get students thinking like founders. We ran four webinars. Then engagement dropped, and it stopped. Just like that.

## What I tried

Start The Up was built around webinars. I picked topics, got people to join, and ran four sessions.

When engagement dropped, it stopped. Looking back, I never built a repeating ritual around it, which I think is the biggest thing I missed. I will be honest: I have more than 50 projects on my drive and 20 to 30 of them are unfinished, and this was one more lesson in finishing.

## What a good student community actually looks like

I have been a member of student communities that worked. The best example for me is **Think Digital**, which I joined by accident and which changed me from an introvert into someone who goes to events every week. What they did, which I would copy:

- **A weekly showcase.** Members show what they built that week. It pushed me to build something every week, even when I had nothing, just to have something to show.
- **Casual practice meets.** Regular, low-pressure meetups where the only rule was to speak in English, rant, whatever. Nobody judged my grammar.
- **Offline gatherings** that made it easy to meet like-minded people in person.
- **A non-judgmental culture.** People felt safe being bad at things in public.

Notice that none of these are one-off events. They are rituals that repeat, and the community lives in the repeating.

## How to start one, step by step

1. **Start with a few founding members.** Growth advice from community builders says to begin with a small group of founders and events before opening up wide. Five committed people beat five hundred strangers in a group.
2. **Write one clear promise.** "A place for first-year students who want to start something" is clear. "A community for everyone interested in startups" is not.
3. **Pick one ritual and keep it.** A weekly showcase, a monthly speaker, a Sunday meet. Choose the smallest thing you can keep doing for six months.
4. **Find speakers and mentors early.** Reach out to founders and seniors, and ask for twenty minutes. Alumni are often more willing to help than you expect.
5. **Plan the handover.** The Wadhwani Foundation argues that what separates lasting student startup hubs from ones that fade is carrying knowledge and leadership forward year after year, so decide early who will run it when you are busy. You can read their piece on [how student communities become startup incubators](https://wadhwanifoundation.org/from-campus-clubs-to-unicorn-startups/).
6. **Use the resources around you.** Your college's entrepreneurship cell, nearby startup events and local communities can lend a venue, speakers and an audience.

## What I would test next time

I do not know for certain why Start The Up faded, so these are things I would try, not conclusions:

- Run a weekly or fortnightly showcase instead of one-off webinars, like the best communities I have seen.
- Share the work with two or three other students early, so it does not depend only on me.
- Keep the first version smaller and finish it, before adding a second idea.

## Why it is still worth trying

Starting a community taught me about organising, speaking to people I did not know, and following something through, even though it did not last. A community also does something a college class rarely does. It gives students an identity, a network, exposure and awareness. That is the same four-part idea from my own talk on product building, and it came from being part of one.

## Questions people ask me

### How do I start a startup community in college?

Start with a few founding members, write one clear promise, pick one repeating ritual, and plan who takes over when you get busy.

### How many people do I need to start?

Very few. A small, committed group is better than a big, silent one.

### Why do student communities die?

From my own experience, they depend too much on one person and have no repeating ritual. Lasting ones carry leadership forward year after year.

### Should I run webinars or in-person meets?

Both can work, but make it something regular. Think Digital's weekly showcase and casual meets worked because they repeated.

### Can a student community help my startup?

It can help with people, feedback and exposure. In my case, being part of one gave me connections and the confidence to talk to strangers.
